1
Vocabulary before choices

Basic concepts

Each term now includes a complete decision context. Read the evidence and prompt first; then reveal the resolved role.

Fact / evidence
Information the text explicitly gives: an observation, result, comparison, condition, or statement.
Context: In a survey of 24 rooftop gardens during the same dry week, shaded plots retained more surface moisture than exposed plots. The survey did not measure plant growth.
Decision target: Identify the explicit result, not a later claim about what the result means.
Resolved role

Evidence: shaded plots retained more surface moisture during the surveyed week.

This is directly reported. “Shade improves plant growth” is not evidence because growth was not measured.

Claim / theory
An idea that the evidence may support, limit, or help complete.
Context: After reporting the moisture pattern, the researchers propose that reduced direct sunlight slows evaporation from the soil surface. They have not yet tested evaporation rate directly.
Decision target: Separate the researchers' proposed explanation from the reported observation.
Resolved role

Claim / theory: reduced direct sunlight slows evaporation.

The sentence proposes why the pattern occurred; it is not itself a measured result.

Inference
A conclusion not stated word for word but strongly supported by the text's facts and logic.
Context: The shaded and exposed plots received the same amount of water. By week's end, the shaded plots retained more moisture. No evaporation sensor was used.
Decision target: Build the smallest supported bridge between the matched watering and the moisture difference.
Resolved role

Inference: shade may have reduced moisture loss in this study.

“May” preserves the unmeasured mechanism; the conclusion does not claim certainty.

Conclusion
The endpoint produced by combining the relevant facts without adding a new premise.
Context: Twelve shaded plots and twelve exposed plots were observed for one week. The shaded plots retained more moisture; plant health and long-term soil change were not measured.
Decision target: State the endpoint that all reported facts permit.
Resolved role

Conclusion: the trial provides evidence of a short-term relationship between shade and soil moisture in the observed plots.

The endpoint keeps the measured variable, duration, and sample.

Assumption
An unstated idea that must be true for a conclusion to work. The safest answer needs the fewest new assumptions.
Context: The rooftop study measured moisture for one week but did not record crop yield. A proposed answer claims that shade improves the yield of every rooftop crop.
Decision target: Name the unsupported bridge required by the proposed answer.
Resolved role

Assumption: greater short-term moisture necessarily improves yield across all crops and conditions.

The study supplies neither a yield outcome nor a universal crop sample.

Scope
The population, place, condition, or time period to which a claim applies.
Context: Researchers monitored twelve rooftop gardens in one city during one unusually dry week. A proposed answer refers to all urban gardens in every season.
Decision target: Restore the evidence boundary the proposed answer erased.
Resolved role

Scope lock: the monitored rooftop gardens, in that city, during the observed dry week.

Sample, place, condition, and time are decision evidence, not background decoration.

Modality
The degree of certainty expressed by words such as may, can, likely, or must.
Context: Moisture differed across plots, but the study was short and did not measure evaporation directly. One answer says shade may reduce moisture loss; another says it always prevents moisture loss.
Decision target: Match the conclusion's certainty to the evidence.
Resolved role

Safe modality: may reduce.

“Always prevents” adds certainty and universality that the trial cannot support.

Association
Two variables change together; the text has not necessarily shown that one caused the other.
Context: In a city survey, people reporting more late-night light exposure also reported shorter sleep. Work schedules and caffeine use were not controlled.
Decision target: State the strongest relationship the observational design permits.
Resolved role

Association: greater late-night light exposure was associated with shorter reported sleep.

Other variables could influence both; the survey does not isolate a cause.

Causation
One factor produces a change in another. A causal claim needs a design that supports direction and rules out plausible alternatives.
Context: Researchers randomly assigned similar seedlings to identical chambers that differed only in light color. After four weeks, the groups differed in average height.
Decision target: Identify why this design can support a causal claim within its tested conditions.
Resolved role

Causal support: random assignment plus one controlled difference links light color to the measured height difference in this experiment.

The claim still stays inside the tested variety, duration, and chamber conditions.

Three repeatable routes

Rule / permission map

Use the lane that matches the text's job, then run the same safety audit.

Supported conclusion

  1. List the explicit facts.
  2. Name their relationship.
  3. Write the minimum conclusion.
  4. Lock scope, time, and degree.
  5. Reject every added assumption.

Logical completion

  1. Read through the blank.
  2. Name the current move.
  3. Restate the cause/contrast/result chain.
  4. Predict the ending broadly.
  5. Match logic before wording.

Final safety audit

  1. Every clause supported?
  2. Same population and conditions?
  3. Same certainty?
  4. No causal upgrade?
  5. No new advice, motive, or agent?
3
Rules in full context

Knowledge points

Each card contains a complete passage, a precise decision job, a resolved answer-like form, and the closest trap.

1

Build only inside the evidence boundary

Concept / rule

Every meaningful word in the conclusion must be licensed by the passage. A plausible real-world idea is still unsupported if the text does not supply the bridge.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

A coastal archive digitized 18,000 photographs. Before digitization, researchers had to visit the archive to inspect most images; afterward, the files could be viewed through an online catalog. The archive has not reported whether more scholarly articles were published after the project.

Decision job: Complete the archive's claim without inventing an outcome that was never measured.

Answer-like form

Digitization made the photographs accessible to researchers who could not visit the archive in person.

Why the reasoning works

The text directly contrasts on-site inspection with online access. It supports a change in access, not a change in research quality, publication counts, or public interest.

Closest trap

Digitization greatly increased the archive's scholarly influence. The idea is possible, but influence was not measured.

2

Make a small logical bridge—not a restatement or a leap

Concept / rule

A useful inference goes slightly beyond a single sentence by combining facts, but it stays close enough that the passage makes the conclusion hard to deny.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

Biologists recorded the first flowering date of the same alpine plant population for twenty years. In years when spring snow melted earlier, flowering also began earlier. Across the record, the interval between snowmelt and first flowering remained nearly constant.

Decision job: State the relationship created by all three facts.

Answer-like form

The timing of snowmelt may help set the flowering schedule of this alpine population.

Why the reasoning works

The answer combines the repeated association and the stable interval. It does not merely repeat “flowering began earlier,” and it does not claim that snowmelt controls flowering everywhere.

Closest trap

Earlier snowmelt will cause all alpine species to flower earlier. This changes one observed population into all species and turns an association into a guaranteed cause.

3

Follow the argument's direction

Concept / rule

For a logical completion, identify the current move—cause, contrast, example, result, or conclusion—before predicting the ending. Topic match alone is not enough.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

Traditional maps of the wetland were updated only after crews completed seasonal field surveys. A new satellite system records water boundaries every three days. Because temporary pools can appear and disappear between field visits, ______.

Decision job: Complete the result already established by the cause chain.

Answer-like form

the satellite system can reveal short-lived changes that the seasonal maps may miss

Why the reasoning works

Seasonal visits leave long gaps; the satellite observes every three days; short-lived pools can occur inside those gaps. The blank therefore needs a detection advantage, expressed cautiously.

Closest trap

field surveys remain the most reliable way to classify wetland plants. This stays on the wetland topic but changes the move from a mapping result to a new recommendation about plants.

4

Choose the conclusion with the fewest added assumptions

Concept / rule

When several statements seem possible, prefer the one fully supported by the stated facts. Each extra cause, motive, forecast, or recommendation is another assumption to justify.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

A museum placed identical descriptions beside two groups of unfamiliar objects. One description group included a short question inviting visitors to compare shapes; the other did not. Visitors who saw the questions spent longer at the display, but the study did not test what they remembered later.

Decision job: Draw the minimum safe inference from the measured result.

Answer-like form

Comparison questions may increase the time visitors spend examining unfamiliar objects.

Why the reasoning works

The answer preserves the intervention, measured outcome, and limited certainty. It does not assume that longer viewing caused stronger memory or greater enjoyment.

Closest trap

Comparison questions improve visitors' long-term learning. That conclusion needs an unmeasured memory outcome and a causal link from viewing time to learning.

5

Lock population, place, condition, and time

Concept / rule

A conclusion cannot silently expand beyond the passage's sample or conditions. Carry the smallest relevant scope phrase into the answer.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

During one unusually dry summer, researchers monitored twelve young maple trees growing beside paved streets in Toronto. Trees receiving an extra weekly watering showed less leaf browning than nearby trees receiving only rainfall. Mature trees and other climates were not studied.

Decision job: Preserve every important scope boundary.

Answer-like form

For young street-side maples in the monitored dry summer, extra weekly watering was associated with less leaf browning.

Why the reasoning works

The answer keeps the age, setting, season, treatment, and observed outcome. Those details are not decoration; they define where the evidence applies.

Closest trap

Weekly watering prevents drought damage in urban trees. The trap drops age, species, city, season, and the limited outcome of leaf browning.

6

Match the text's degree of certainty

Concept / rule

Keep the passage's modality. Evidence that something may, can, or is likely to occur does not support must, will, always, or proves.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

In laboratory tanks, juvenile fish exposed to a low-frequency hum entered a shelter sooner on average than fish in quiet tanks. Individual responses varied widely, and the researchers note that currents and predators could change the pattern in the wild.

Decision job: State a conclusion no stronger than the evidence.

Answer-like form

A low-frequency hum may influence shelter-seeking behavior in juvenile fish under laboratory conditions.

Why the reasoning works

May matches variable responses and preserves the laboratory setting. The experiment supports an influence in these tanks, not a universal behavioral rule in nature.

Closest trap

Low-frequency sound always causes juvenile fish to seek shelter. Always erases variation, and the claim ignores the study's explicit field limitation.

7

Do not turn association into causation

Concept / rule

If a study only observes that two variables move together, conclude that they are related. Claim causation only when the design supports the direction and controls alternatives.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

A city survey found that neighborhoods with denser tree cover also had lower average afternoon surface temperatures. The survey did not randomly assign trees, and neighborhoods differed in building height, pavement, and distance from the river.

Decision job: Respect what the observational design can establish.

Answer-like form

In the surveyed neighborhoods, denser tree cover was associated with lower afternoon surface temperatures.

Why the reasoning works

The variables covary, but other neighborhood features could affect temperature. The text does not isolate tree cover as the sole cause.

Closest trap

Planting trees will necessarily lower afternoon temperatures in every neighborhood. This converts correlation into guaranteed causation and expands beyond the sample.

8

Preserve valid negative meaning

Concept / rule

A correct answer may restate a positive claim as a logically equivalent negative one. Track who or what is negated so that the meaning does not reverse.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

The early printing process could reproduce bold outlines reliably, but fine gray shading often disappeared. As a result, illustrators designed many images around strong contrasts rather than subtle tonal changes.

Decision job: Express the same limitation in a negative form.

Answer-like form

The process was not well suited to images whose effect depended on delicate gradations of tone.

Why the reasoning works

“Fine gray shading often disappeared” and “not well suited to delicate gradations” express the same constraint. The negative form changes wording, not logic.

Closest trap

The process could not reproduce images with strong contrasts. This attaches the negative to the feature the process handled reliably and reverses the evidence.

9

Untangle double negatives before judging

Concept / rule

Translate nested negatives into a simple positive or limited claim. Then test that simplified meaning against the passage.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

Reviewers of a new translation disagree about its rhythm, but nearly all acknowledge that it preserves the poem's unusual sequence of images. One critic writes that the translation is “not without fidelity to the poem's visual structure.”

Decision job: Resolve the critic's double-negative claim.

Answer-like form

The critic concedes that the translation has at least some fidelity to the poem's visual structure.

Why the reasoning works

Not without means “with some,” not “perfectly faithful.” The wording signals a limited positive concession that fits the broader reviewer consensus.

Closest trap

The critic argues that the translation lacks fidelity to the poem's images. This reads only the surface negative and misses that without is also negative.

10

Reject half-right, half-wrong conclusions

Concept / rule

An answer fails if any important clause exceeds or contradicts the evidence. Familiar wording in the first half cannot rescue an unsupported second half.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

Engineers tested a clay coating on small roof tiles. Coated tiles absorbed less water during a two-hour spray test, but both coated and uncoated tiles cracked after repeated freezing. The study did not measure performance on full roofs.

Decision job: Keep both parts of the conclusion inside the results.

Answer-like form

The coating reduced short-term water absorption in the tested tiles but did not prevent cracking during repeated freezing.

Why the reasoning works

Both clauses correspond to measured outcomes. The answer preserves the contrast between one benefit and one limit.

Closest trap

The coating reduced water absorption and made roofs resistant to winter damage. The first part is supported; the second changes small-tile cracking results into untested roof performance.

11

Reject the right topic with the wrong logical move

Concept / rule

An answer can repeat every major noun and still fail if it reverses cause and effect, supplies a contrast where a result is needed, or changes a conclusion into an example.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

The canyon's north wall receives less direct sunlight than its south wall. Snow therefore remains on the north wall later into spring. Because meltwater becomes available there later, plants on the north wall begin their main growth period after those on the south wall, ______.

Decision job: Finish the chain with a consequence of the later start.

Answer-like form

so the two plant communities can reach comparable growth stages at different times

Why the reasoning works

The chain runs sunlight → snowmelt timing → water timing → growth timing. The completion summarizes a timing difference without adding a new cause.

Closest trap

even though both canyon walls receive the same amount of direct sunlight. The nouns match, but the statement contradicts the first sentence and supplies the wrong relationship.

12

Do not invent a recommendation, motive, or new agent

Concept / rule

An inference completes the text's existing claim. It cannot introduce what someone should do, why a person acted, or what an unmentioned group believes unless the passage establishes it.

Complete SAT-style demonstration

A regional library extended weekend hours at four branches. Visits during the new hours were highest at the branch farthest from the city center, where public transit service ends early on weekdays. The library did not survey visitors about their reasons for attending.

Decision job: Conclude only what the attendance pattern permits.

Answer-like form

Weekend hours may be especially useful for patrons of the branch with limited weekday-evening transit access.

Why the reasoning works

The cautious inference connects the branch condition with the observed attendance pattern. It does not claim to know each visitor's motive or prescribe a system-wide policy.

Closest trap

The library should replace weekday evening hours with weekend hours at every branch. This adds a recommendation, expands from four branches to every branch, and assumes the reason for attendance.

What the evidence allows

SAT permission / illegal-use chart

Both columns use complete claims so the boundary is visible, not merely named.

Move Permitted conclusion Illegal extension
Paraphrase the same meaning Legal

A seed's outer coat remained intact after brief heating; therefore, brief heating did not visibly damage the coat.

 Illegal

Brief heating made the seed more likely to germinate. Germination was never measured.
Combine two or three stated facts Legal

The sensor samples hourly, brief spikes can occur between samples, so some spikes may go unrecorded.

 Illegal

The sensor is inaccurate. Missing short events does not prove wrong readings when it records.
Carry a cautious modality Legal

The pattern may indicate that warmer nights delay leaf color change in this sample.

 Illegal

Warmer nights always prevent leaves from changing color.
Preserve population and condition Legal

Among the monitored first-year students, weekly planning sessions were associated with fewer late submissions.

 Illegal

Planning sessions eliminate late work for all students.
State an association as an association Legal

Higher soil salinity was associated with slower seedling growth in the surveyed plots.

 Illegal

Salt caused the slower growth; the observational survey did not isolate a cause.
Use an equivalent negative paraphrase Legal

The pigment was not stable under prolonged ultraviolet exposure.

 Illegal

The pigment could not produce vivid color; color intensity was not the measured limit.
Preserve an explicit contrast or limitation Legal

The filter removed larger particles but did not reduce dissolved metals.

 Illegal

The filter purified the water; this erases the stated limit.
Finish the current logic chain Legal

Shorter intervals reduce the chance that a brief event occurs between observations; therefore, they can improve detection of brief events.

 Illegal

Researchers should always collect data continuously; this adds a recommendation and an absolute.
5
Mistake first, repair second

High-frequency trap library

Recognize the visible lure, then uncover the failure, repair, and portable rule.

1. Topic match

Mistake: “The new satellite system is more expensive than seasonal field surveys.”

Failure: The conclusion repeats the passage's nouns but does not perform the required logical move.

Repair: “The satellite system can reveal short-lived wetland changes that seasonal maps may miss.”

Portable rule: Right subject, wrong reasoning job = wrong answer.

2. Outside knowledge

Mistake: “Comparison questions improve visitors’ long-term memory.”

Failure: The statement is reasonable in real life but the passage never provides its premise.

Repair: “Comparison questions may increase the time visitors spend examining unfamiliar objects.”

Portable rule: Plausible is not the same as supported.

3. Causal leap

Mistake: “Denser tree cover caused lower surface temperatures in the surveyed neighborhoods.”

Failure: An association is rewritten as proof that one variable produced the other.

Repair: “In the surveyed neighborhoods, denser tree cover was associated with lower surface temperatures.”

Portable rule: No causal verb without causal evidence.

4. Some-to-all expansion

Mistake: “Weekly watering prevents drought damage in all urban trees.”

Failure: A limited sample or qualified pattern becomes a universal rule.

Repair: “During the dry summer, weekly watering was associated with less leaf browning in the young Toronto maples studied.”

Portable rule: Scope words are evidence locks.

5. May-to-will upgrade

Mistake: “Low-frequency sound will always cause juvenile fish to seek shelter.”

Failure: A cautious possibility becomes a certain prediction.

Repair: “Under the laboratory conditions tested, low-frequency sound may influence juvenile fish to seek shelter.”

Portable rule: Never strengthen certainty for free.

6. Wrong time or condition

Mistake: “Wild juvenile fish respond to low-frequency sound in the same way as the fish in the laboratory tanks.”

Failure: A result from one season, setting, or treatment is projected into another.

Repair: “The response was observed in juvenile fish housed in the laboratory tanks used in the study.”

Portable rule: Same variable does not mean same condition.

7. New recommendation

Mistake: “The library should replace weekday evening hours with weekend hours at every branch.”

Failure: The passage explains what happened; the conclusion tells someone what should happen.

Repair: “The survey may help the library identify when the respondents are most likely to use extended hours.”

Portable rule: Description does not automatically license advice.

8. Half-right / half-wrong

Mistake: “The coating reduced water absorption and made full roofs resistant to winter damage.”

Failure: The first clause is supported, but a second clause adds an unmeasured result.

Repair: “On the roof tiles tested, the coating reduced water absorption but did not prevent cracking.”

Portable rule: One unsupported clause invalidates the whole answer.

9. Reversal

Mistake: “Later snowmelt causes the canyon’s north wall to receive less sunlight.”

Failure: The answer swaps cause and effect, earlier and later, or the group with the stronger result.

Repair: “Because the canyon’s north wall receives less sunlight, snow there tends to melt later.”

Portable rule: Correct nouns in the wrong direction are still wrong.

10. Recycled language

Mistake: “Strong headwinds exhausted the tagged thrushes during their first night of migration.”

Failure: Words lifted from the passage create familiarity while the relationship between them is altered.

Repair: “Strong headwinds may have limited how far the tagged thrushes traveled on their first night; the study did not measure energy use.”

Portable rule: Shared wording is not shared logic.

6
Resolved end-to-end reasoning

Full-method demonstrations

Each method runs from a complete passage to a scope-checked conclusion.

Fact-to-Conclusion Chain

Complete passage

Researchers attached lightweight tags to migrating thrushes at two coastal stopover sites. Birds leaving the northern site encountered strong headwinds during the first night and paused at inland forests the next day. Birds leaving the southern site encountered weaker winds and traveled farther before their next recorded stop. The tags measured movement, not energy use.

Decision job: Infer the relationship supported by the movement records.

Full method trace
  1. Facts: two departure sites; stronger versus weaker winds; shorter versus longer first-night travel; energy use unmeasured.
  2. Relationship: the observed travel distance varies with the wind conditions encountered after departure.
  3. Minimum conclusion: headwinds may limit how far these tagged thrushes travel during the first night.
  4. Locks: may, these tagged thrushes, first night; no claim about energy or all migration.
  5. Trap rejection: “headwinds exhausted the birds” invents an internal state the tags did not measure.
Resolved conclusion

Strong headwinds may have limited the first-night travel distance of the tagged thrushes leaving the northern site.

Logical-Blank Completion

Complete passage

For decades, historians dated the harbor wall by comparing its masonry with dated buildings nearby. A newly recovered tax record lists payments for transporting the wall's distinctive stone in 1742, nine years earlier than the date scholars had proposed. Because the record names both the material and the construction site, ______.

Decision job: Complete the result established by the new evidence.

Full method trace
  1. Current move: old estimate contrasted with a newly dated documentary record.
  2. Evidence chain: named stone + named site + 1742 payment → construction activity by 1742.
  3. Predicted ending: the wall may need an earlier date than the comparison-based estimate.
  4. Match: keep may because payment evidence supports but may not prove final completion date.
  5. Trap rejection: a claim about the stone's durability is on-topic but outside the chain.
Resolved conclusion

the wall's construction likely began earlier than the comparison-based estimate suggested

Minimum Safe Inference

Complete passage

A university tested two reminder messages for optional tutoring appointments. Students randomly assigned a message that named the appointment time and included a calendar link attended at a higher rate than students receiving a message that named only the time. The study did not separate the effects of the extra link from the extra visual emphasis around it.

Decision job: Choose a conclusion that preserves both the randomized result and the stated limitation.

Full method trace
  1. Evidence: random assignment; combined time-plus-link message; higher attendance.
  2. Limitation: the link and its visual emphasis changed together.
  3. Minimum claim: the combined message format increased attendance in this test.
  4. Do not isolate: the calendar link alone cannot be credited.
  5. Do not expand: no claim about grades, motivation, or every type of appointment.
Resolved conclusion

In this test, the time-plus-calendar-link message increased tutoring attendance, though the study cannot show that the link alone produced the difference.

7
Cover, retrieve, verify

One-page recall deck

Each front states a taught rule. Each back gives a compact resolved example.

Card 1 Evidence boundary

Every meaningful word in an inference needs passage support.

Resolved example: Archive files became viewable online; therefore, remote access increased. Publication impact remains unknown.

Card 2 Small bridge

Combine facts into the least new conclusion.

Resolved example: Stable snowmelt-to-flowering intervals suggest that snowmelt timing may help set flowering time in the observed population.

Card 3 Argument direction

Name cause, contrast, example, result, or conclusion before matching.

Resolved example: Long gaps can miss brief pools; frequent images can detect changes the gaps miss.

Card 4 Minimum assumptions

Prefer the conclusion that adds no new outcome, motive, or policy.

Resolved example: Longer viewing supports a viewing-time claim—not a memory claim.

Card 5 Scope lock

Keep population, place, condition, and time.

Resolved example: Young Toronto street maples in one dry summer do not represent all urban trees.

Card 6 Degree lock

May/can/likely cannot become must/will/always.

Resolved example: Variable tank responses support may influence, not always causes.

Card 7 Association / causation

Observed co-variation is not automatically a causal effect.

Resolved example: Tree cover was associated with cooler surfaces; other neighborhood features may matter.

Card 8 Negative paraphrase

A negative form can preserve a positive limitation.

Resolved example: Fine shading disappeared → not well suited to delicate tonal gradations.

Card 9 Double negative

Simplify the negatives before testing meaning.

Resolved example: Not without fidelity = with at least some fidelity, not perfect fidelity.

Card 10 Clause audit

Every clause of an answer must be supported.

Resolved example: Reduced water absorption is supported; roof-wide winter resistance is not.

Card 11 Right topic, right move

Matching nouns do not repair a reversed or irrelevant relationship.

Resolved example: Draw the arrow: sunlight → snowmelt → water timing → growth timing.

Card 12 No new task

Do not invent a recommendation, motive, or new agent.

Resolved example: Attendance patterns may show usefulness; they do not automatically dictate system-wide scheduling.

Five-second final audit

Before accepting an inference

Facts: Can I point to every premise? · Direction: Does the conclusion finish the passage's move? · Scope: Same people, place, condition, and time? · Degree: Same certainty? · Assumptions: Did I add a cause, motive, outcome, or recommendation?
